### [INTRO — Friendly, Welcoming Tone]

Hey there, and welcome! This is part one of a two-part lesson on BIM closeout — the process of submitting your models at the end of the job so they can be approved and archived.

Let me give you the one sentence that explains why you should care about this: closeout is tied to final payment. On most projects, the models you produced during coordination and construction have to be submitted and approved before that last check gets released. So this isn't paperwork you do "eventually" — it's the gate between you and getting paid in full.

Part one, today, is all about preparation — getting your files genuinely ready before you submit. Part two will cover the actual submittal and the review workflow. And here's my honest coaching: 90 percent of closeout pain comes from submitting files that weren't ready. Nail the prep, and the rest is easy. Let's get into it.

---

### [SECTION 1 — What You're Actually Submitting]

First, what does closeout even consist of? Two kinds of files, and they travel together.

One — your coordination files. The Navisworks files: the N-W-C and N-W-D files.

Two — the matching source files. Whatever you authored from: the D-W-G, the R-V-T, the I-F-C, or Tekla files.

The key principle: source files and their coordination files get submitted together, as a matched set. A D-W-G goes in with its N-W-C. A Revit model goes in with all of its N-W-Cs. They're a package — never one without the other. Remember that, because it's the single most common thing people get wrong.

---

### [SECTION 2 — The Requirements Checklist]

So what makes a file "ready"? Here's the standard a file has to meet before it's worth submitting.

It has to be named correctly, following the project's naming standard — the right building code, level, discipline code, sector, and so on. When applicable, the layers have to follow the project CAD standard. All the latest design revisions and R-F-Is have to be incorporated — the model has to reflect the final design, not last month's. Your coordination issues and your field observations have to be cleared or formally adjudicated — not left hanging. And the model needs to match the field within the project tolerance, typically about plus-or-minus one inch.

Hear the theme: a closeout file is a finished, accurate, fully-reconciled record. It's not a work-in-progress. If any of those things are still open, the file isn't ready yet.

---

### [SECTION 3 — The Prerequisites: Before You Even Think About Submitting]

Now let's go deeper on the prerequisites, because there are a few that trip people up.

First — you have to have taken full ownership of all your scope. That means every I-F-C, every D-R-B, every R-F-I is accounted for in your model. A good habit here: run a comparison of your modeled content against the design model, so you can confirm nothing's missing.

Second — and this one's easy to forget — no duplicated content. Make sure you haven't doubled up geometry with yourself or with another trade. The classic example is modeling the same support that your third-party contractor or a neighboring trade also modeled. Two of the same thing is a closeout problem.

Third — all your field observations have to be closed, both the field fixes and the model fixes. All your area or sector models need their fabrication releases complete, with as-builts provided. And all your coordination issues need to be fully resolved — in a reconciled state — not sitting open, conditional, or awaiting a response.

And here's a responsibility point people miss: if you brought in a third-party contractor who created files under their own author code, getting those files closed out is still your responsibility. You can have them do it through your company, or you can take ownership and finish it yourself — but either way, it's on you. Their loose ends become your loose ends at closeout.

---

### [SECTION 4 — The AutoCAD Checklist]

Now the discipline-specific prep. If you're submitting AutoCAD files, here's your checklist.

Your layer naming, colors, and line types all have to match the project CAD standard. There should be no frozen layers — and you purge out any unused or irrelevant layers. Then run a full purge on the file.

And here's a logic rule worth internalizing: all your layers should be turned on. Because if a layer is turned off since it isn't relevant content — then it shouldn't be in the model at all. "Off because it doesn't belong" means "delete it," not "hide it." Finally, make sure all content is aligned to the project grids and coordinates.

---

### [SECTION 5 — The Revit Checklist]

If you're submitting Revit, there's a longer checklist, and it's worth doing in order. Let me walk it.

Start by confirming the file is on the project-specified Revit version — don't assume, check. Confirm the file name is correct. Acquire the shared coordinates. Make sure Revit grids are present in every file. Remove all the X-REF links. Add the package name onto all your 3D objects. Remove any unnecessary worksets. Clear out in-place families. Clear out design options. Work through and resolve your Revit warnings. Remove all 2D content. Ungroup your groups and break down all assemblies. And finally — purge all.

That's a lot, I know. But notice what it's really doing: it's stripping the model down to clean, pure, coordinated 3D content with nothing extra hiding inside it. A closeout model should be lean and honest. Every item on that list removes something that could confuse the people who archive and rely on this model years from now.

### [OUTRO]

So that's preparation. You know what you're submitting — source plus coordination files, together. You know the requirements — named right, revisions in, issues reconciled, accurate to about an inch. And you know the AutoCAD and Revit checklists that get a file truly clean.

Do this work before you touch the submit button, and part two — actually submitting and getting approved — becomes smooth. That's where we're headed next. See you there.
