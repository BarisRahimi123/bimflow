Welcome back! In this module, we're covering the A-9 Change Management document.

This is one of the most critical documents for BIM coordination, because it answers a question you'll face every single day: How much can my model deviate from the design?

In a perfect world, everything would be modeled exactly as designed. But we don't live in a perfect world. There are clashes to resolve. There are constructability issues. Sometimes you need to move things.

The A-9 tells you how much movement is acceptable - and when you need to go through a formal change process.

Let's start with the basics. The A-9 contains tolerance tables organized by discipline. You'll find tables for Process, Mechanical, Electrical, Structural, and Architectural.

Each table lists different element types and their approved tolerances.

But here's the key insight that many people miss: The tolerance depends not just on WHAT the element is, but also on WHERE it is located.

Let me explain with an example.

Say you have a 4-inch process pipe. What's the tolerance?

Well, it depends. Is the pipe in a subfab lateral rack? Is it in an EOR engineered rack? Or is it an individual pipe run out in the field?

If it's in a subfab lateral rack, the tolerance is ZERO. Zero inches. No deviation allowed. This is a critical area.

If it's in an EOR engineered rack, the tolerance is one-half inch.

If it's an individual pipe run not in a rack, the tolerance is 6 inches.

Same pipe. Same size. But three completely different tolerances based on location.

This is why you need to understand the A-9 deeply. You can't just memorize one number. You need to know how location affects tolerance.

Let me walk you through the key tolerance categories.

For Process discipline, here are the main ones:

Subfab lateral racks - zero tolerance. These are critical. The design must be followed exactly.

Process piping in EOR engineered racks - one-half inch tolerance.

Utility mains and sub-mains - 1 inch tolerance.

Equipment and panel datums - one-half inch tolerance, except subfab which is zero.

Equipment feeders and utility branches to equipment - 6 inch tolerance.

Individual pipe runs including branch fitting locations - 6 inch tolerance.

In-line component location and orientation - 6 inches axially.

EOR designed pipe supports - 6 inch tolerance.

And all remaining components - 2 inch tolerance as a default.

For Mechanical discipline, the pattern is similar:

Subfab lateral rack exhaust laterals - zero tolerance.

Mechanical equipment - zero tolerance, but with a note. The location in the model is for basis of design only. Final location is owned by the contractor.

Ductwork mains and branches - 6 inch tolerance.

Mechanical piping in EOR racks - 1 inch tolerance.

Mechanical piping not in racks - 6 inch tolerance.

Plumbing mains and drains - 6 inch tolerance.

Plumbing branches - 12 inch tolerance. That's a full foot.

Plumbing underground - 2 inch tolerance.

For Electrical discipline:

Raceways in subfab lateral racks - 1 inch tolerance.

Raceways in EOR engineered racks - 2 inch tolerance.

Raceways not in racks - 6 inch tolerance.

Major equipment datums like switchgear and transformers - one-half inch tolerance.

Individual conduits - 6 inch tolerance.

For Structural discipline:

Subfab lateral racks - zero tolerance.

Main building steel and structure - zero tolerance.

Cleanroom ceiling grid - zero tolerance.

EOR designed pipe supports - 6 inch tolerance.

Now, what do these tolerances actually mean for you?

If you need to move something within the approved tolerance, you can do it. You don't need to file a formal change request. Just make sure you document it during coordination.

But if you need to move something beyond the approved tolerance, that's a different story. You need to follow the change management process. You'll need to get approval before making that deviation.

Here's a practical tip. When you're resolving a clash, first check the A-9. Can you resolve it within the tolerance? If yes, great - make the move and document it. If no, you need to escalate.

Another important concept: Some elements have zero tolerance. That means the design model is the final word. You cannot deviate. If there's a clash with a zero-tolerance element, something else has to move - not the zero-tolerance item.

Examples of zero-tolerance items include:
Subfab lateral racks,
Main building steel,
Cleanroom ceiling grid,
And architectural building shell.

These are your constraints. Everything else has to work around them.

Let's also talk about the phrase "LOD may increase but locations should not deviate."

This appears in the A-9 for some elements. It means you can add more detail to the model - like going from a simple box to a detailed piece of equipment - but the location must stay the same. The centerpoint, the datum, the position - those are fixed.

Alright, let's summarize the key takeaways.

Number one: The A-9 defines how much your model can deviate from the design.

Number two: Tolerance depends on BOTH the element type AND the location. A pipe in a subfab rack has zero tolerance. The same pipe as an individual run has 6 inch tolerance.

Number three: Zero tolerance means no deviation allowed. Period.

Number four: If you can resolve a clash within tolerance, do it and document it. If you can't, escalate through the change process.

Number five: Know your constraints. Zero-tolerance elements don't move. Everything else works around them.

Number six: Always check the A-9 before making changes during coordination.

That's the A-9 Change Management document. Master this, and you'll know exactly how much flexibility you have when resolving issues.
