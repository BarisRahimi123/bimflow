# BIM Modeling and Coordination Guide - Training Script
## For ElevenLabs Voiceover

---

### [INTRO - Friendly, Welcoming Tone]

Hey there! Welcome to this training module on BIM Modeling and Coordination.

I'm going to walk you through everything you need to know about how design documents work on this project, and what's expected from you as a subcontractor. Don't worry - I'll keep it simple and straightforward.

By the end of this training, you'll understand exactly where to find design information, what documents to follow, and how to deliver your BIM models the right way.

Let's dive in!

---

### [SECTION 1 - The Big Picture]

Okay, first things first. Let me share the most important thing you need to understand about this project.

The design is NOT delivered to you as one complete BIM model.

Let me say that again because it's really important.

There is no single model that contains everything you need. The design is spread across multiple sources - models, specs, details, and drawings.

Your job is to take information from all these different sources, combine them, interpret them, and create your own detailed construction model.

Think of it like a puzzle. The engineer gives you pieces from different boxes. You need to find the right pieces, put them together, and build something that actually works in the real world.

This is why understanding the document structure is so critical. You need to know where to look for what.

---

### [SECTION 2 - The Two Types of Documents]

Now, let's talk about the documents you'll be working with. There are two main categories.

Category one: Procedural Documents. These are the rule books. They tell you HOW to do things and WHAT requirements to follow.

Here are the key procedural documents you need to know:

First, the HCC Building Information Modeling Requirements. This is Hoffman's rulebook for BIM.

Second, the HCC BIM HPEP. Another Hoffman document with execution requirements.

Third, the Jacobs A-7 LOD Matrix. This one is super important. It tells you what Level of Development is expected for each element in your model.

Fourth, the Jacobs A-11 Deliverables Format Table. This tells you whether something is delivered in 2D or 3D.

Fifth, the Jacobs A-9 Change Management. This document tells you what deviations from the design are acceptable, and how much tolerance you have.

And sixth, the Applied Materials CAD Standards.

Keep these documents handy. You'll be referring to them a lot.

---

### [SECTION 3 - Design Documents]

Category two: Design Documents. These are the actual design deliverables that show you WHAT to build.

Let's go through them.

First, you have Design Models, or DMs. These are certified models in Navisworks NWD format, and they come with a PDF stamp sheet.

But remember - these design models are not at the full level of detail you need for construction. You'll need to add more detail based on other documents.

Second, you have IFC Documents and Certified Sheets in PDF format. This includes:
- General Arrangement Plans showing equipment and device locations
- P and IDs - your Piping and Instrumentation Diagrams
- Distribution Schematics
- Circuiting Diagrams
- Specifications
- Details
- And any scope that Jacobs delivers in 2D only, as listed in the A-11 table

Third, you have RFIs - Requests for Information - which provide clarifications.

And fourth, sometimes you'll get pre-IFC or work-in-progress versions for early coordination. But only use these when you're authorized to do so.

---

### [SECTION 4 - Your Responsibilities]

Alright, now let's talk about what YOU need to do. This is the important part, so pay close attention.

Responsibility number one: Understand how the design is delivered.

You need to look at the A-11 Deliverables Format Table and the A-7 LOD Matrix to understand what level of detail is provided in the design models, and what you need to add.

Here's a key insight: No single set of models or documents gives you the complete picture. You have to combine information from multiple sources to create a model that's fully constructible and coordinated.

Also, there's an order of precedence for design documents. This is explained in the HCC BIM Execution Requirements. When documents conflict, you need to know which one takes priority.

---

### [SECTION 5 - Construction Models]

Responsibility number two: Provide Construction Models at the right level of detail.

Here's something critical: Hoffman will NOT accept the engineer's Design Models submitted as your own Construction Models.

You cannot just take the design model and call it done. You need to create your OWN Construction Model with:
- Accurate modeling of products and parts per your approved submittals
- The level of detail specified in the HCC BIM Execution Requirements

Also, you must follow the design model routing whenever possible. The A-9 Change Management document and BIM CPEP Appendix 6.1 tell you what deviations are acceptable and how to resolve clashes with the design model.

---

### [SECTION 6 - Coordination]

Responsibility number three: Participate in BIM coordination.

This is not optional. Here's what's expected:

You must attend BIM area coordination meetings. These happen at least twice a week.

You must clash your models and document any issues in BIM Track, the issue tracking platform.

You must fix issues that are assigned to you.

You must coordinate with other subcontractors working in the same area - both in meetings and directly.

And here's a reality check: Creating a fully coordinated, clash-free model takes multiple iterations. This doesn't happen overnight. Plan for this when you're allocating your resources and scheduling your work.

---

### [SECTION 7 - IFF Signoff]

Responsibility number four: IFF all your BIM models.

IFF stands for Issued for Fabrication.

Before you install anything in the field, your Construction Model must be IFF signed off.

And here's the other piece: You need to verify that the field installation actually follows your BIM model. If there are approved field deviations, you need to re-model those changes and resubmit for IFF approval.

The model and the field must match.

---

### [SECTION 8 - Third Party Detailers]

One more thing. If you're using a third-party BIM detailing company, you are still responsible.

You must provide supervision, engineering support, and quality assurance to any company you hire. You can outsource the work, but you cannot outsource the responsibility.

---

### [CLOSING - Encouraging Tone]

Alright, let's wrap this up with the key takeaways.

Number one: The design comes from multiple sources. You need to find, interpret, and combine information to create your construction model.

Number two: Know your documents. The A-7 tells you LOD requirements. The A-9 tells you acceptable tolerances. The A-11 tells you 2D versus 3D deliverables.

Number three: You must create your own Construction Model. Don't just submit the design model.

Number four: Coordination is required. Attend meetings, clash your models, fix your issues, work with other trades.

Number five: IFF everything before you build it. And if the field deviates, update your model.

I know this might seem like a lot, but once you get into the rhythm of it, it becomes second nature.

If you have any questions, reach out to your project BIM lead. They're here to help.

Good luck, and let's build something great together!
