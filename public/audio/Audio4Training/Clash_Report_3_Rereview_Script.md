# Clash Reporting for IFF — Part 3: The Rereview
## Clash Report Training, Part 3 — Training Script for ElevenLabs

> Bracketed headers are screen cues only — for syncing the software screenshots. Paste ONLY the narration text underneath into ElevenLabs.

---

### [INTRO — Friendly, Welcoming Tone]

Welcome to the final part of our clash reporting series.

So here's the situation. You ran your first review in part two. You sent issues back to the trade. They went off and fixed things, and now they've resubmitted the same I-F-F. Your job is to look again — but you don't start from scratch. You build on what you already did. That's the rereview, and doing it cleanly is what keeps a project from going in circles.

Let me walk you through it. There are two stages: getting set up to rerun, and then working the rereview itself.

---

### [SECTION 1 — Refreshing Your File]

First thing, always: open the I-F-F N-W-F and relink it with the latest dated N-W-D. This sounds obvious, but it's everything. If you rereview against the old model, you're reviewing yesterday's work and your whole rereview is worthless. So relink to the newest model first, every time.

---

### [SECTION 2 — Separating Old from New]

Now a clever bit of housekeeping that makes the whole rereview readable. In the Saved Viewpoints dialog, open the I-F-F folder and create a new folder called "Already in BIM Track" — if you haven't already.

Here's the point. Everything you found last time gets moved into "Already in BIM Track." That way, there's a clear wall between what was already known and what's genuinely new this time around. When you can see old-versus-new at a glance, you can actually tell whether the trade made progress.

---

### [SECTION 3 — Re-Running Your QA/QC]

Before you rerun the report, you re-do your quality checks — the same ones from part one. Two things.

One — Q-A-Q-C the selection set, following the exact same steps as the I-F-F search set and selection set setup from part one. Two — Q-A-Q-C the clash report selections, the C-M batches, again referring back to the part one setup.

Then you rerun the clash report by clicking "Update All." You're not rebuilding anything — you're refreshing the same well-built setup against the new model.

---

### [SECTION 4 — Reading the Rereview]

Here's the key insight that makes a rereview fast. After you rerun, any clash that falls outside your existing "Approved Non-Issues" and "Actual Issues" folders is, by definition, brand new — it wasn't there in the last review. So the software is doing the old-versus-new sorting for you.

You handle those new clashes exactly like part two — group them into one folder and dissect them, sorting into Approved Non-Issues and Actual Issues. But with one twist: put a date at the end of these new folders. So you'd have "Approved Non-Issues" with today's date, and "Actual Issues" with today's date. That date stamp tells you, and anyone after you, whether an issue was caught in the first review or showed up in the rereview. It's a paper trail built right into the folder names.

---

### [SECTION 5 — The Decision: Send Back or Proceed]

Once the rereview is done, you make a call. There are two outcomes.

Outcome one — send it back. If the previous issues still aren't fixed, or if this rereview turned up new problems that need real correction by the trade, it goes back to them. And be realistic about this: if you send it back, expect to run the rereview process at least one more time. That's normal — it's the loop working as designed.

Outcome two — proceed. If the previous issues are genuinely fixed, and the rereview didn't surface anything new that's a real problem, then you proceed with the I-F-F. Green light.

---

### [SECTION 6 — The Conditional-Issue Rule]

One last rule, and don't skip it, because it applies no matter which way you decided.

Always document the conditional issues. These are the small things — like tagging problems or minor items — that aren't serious enough to hold up the I-F-F, but still need to get fixed. You let the I-F-F move forward, but you write those items down and you hold the trade to fixing them, typically within a week or two.

Why does this matter? Because "not bad enough to stop the I-F-F" is not the same as "ignore it forever." Conditional issues are how the small stuff gets tracked instead of quietly disappearing. Document them, set the expectation, follow up.

---

### [OUTRO]

And that completes the rereview — and our whole clash reporting series.

Let's recap the journey. In part one you set up your sets, colors, and clash report. In part two you ran the review and sorted real issues from noise. And today you learned to rereview cleanly — relink to the latest model, wall off what's already known, date your new folders, decide send-back versus proceed, and always track the conditional items.

That's the full clash workflow, start to finish. Great work getting through all three — go put it to use.
