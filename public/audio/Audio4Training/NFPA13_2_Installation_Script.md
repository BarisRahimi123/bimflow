### [INTRO — Warm, but Serious]

Welcome back. This is part two of our sprinkler head blockage training, and now we move from the model into the field — where the pipe actually goes up.

In part one you learned the clearances: 12 inches off obstructions, the three-times rule, and that protected 24-by-12 zone around every head. Today is about protecting those clearances when you've got a tool in your hand and a deadline over your shoulder. This is where good intentions either become a safe building — or a punchlist a mile long. Let's get into it.

---

### [SECTION 1 — What We Do NOT Do]

Let me start with the hard "don'ts," because these are the habits that cause the damage.

Don't move things on your own because of a field condition — like hitting rebar — or just to get a cleaner alignment around an embed. Those little unilateral moves are exactly how a head ends up blocked.

Remember the rule: we install based on the I-F-F construction model. That coordinated model is the agreement. You don't quietly depart from it.

And please — don't assume nobody will notice. These floors get walked by Intel, by Jacobs, by Hoffman, and by your own BIM field Q-A. Somebody will see it. The only question is whether it gets caught now, cheaply, or later, expensively.

Which leads to the line this whole section is built around, in capital letters: we must communicate in the field. If something doesn't fit, you speak up — you don't improvise.

---

### [SECTION 2 — What We DO — and the 1-Inch Rule]

So what's the right way? Field coordination has to be part of the install from the very start. Think of it as two phases: BIM I-F-F coordination is phase one, and the field installation is phase two. Phase two isn't a fresh start — it's the faithful execution of phase one.

And here's the number that defines "faithful": contractually, there's a one-inch tolerance between the I-F-F construction model and what you install in the field. One inch. That's your envelope. Drift outside it and you're out of agreement — and possibly back into a blocked clearance zone.

Two more real-world situations to handle. First, field modifications: R-F-Is and D-R-Bs can change systems that are already installed — so when those land, you coordinate the impact in the field, not after. And second, non-modeled content: some things, like pull boxes, may not be in the model at all — but if there's fire protection nearby, they still have to be coordinated. "It wasn't in the model" is not an excuse to let it block a head.

---

### [SECTION 3 — Why This Is Deadly Serious]

Now I'm going to shift tone for a minute, because you need to understand what you're working around.

Treat the fire protection as live at all times. Always. A single broken head discharges around 84 gallons per minute — and over a 20-minute response, that's roughly 1,680 gallons pouring out. Picture that flooding the space you're standing in, around live tools and equipment.

And it's worse than just water. The water sitting in those pipes is black, and it stinks. If you damage a head while installing pipe or conduit, the consequences are real — to people and to expensive tools. So when you're working near a head, you're not working near a decoration. You're working near a charged, pressurized, life-safety device. Respect it accordingly.

There's a cost story here too. The material notes that Intel absorbed real extra costs fixing these conflicts and obstructions across the earlier D1X factories — Mod 1, Mod 2, and Mod 3. This isn't theoretical. It's happened, it's been expensive, and this training exists so it doesn't happen again.

---

### [SECTION 4 — Whose Job Is It?]

So whose responsibility is all this? The answer is short: everyone. Every person on the team is responsible for reviewing the installed work for code compliance.

And here's the consequence of looking away. Ignoring a problem doesn't make it disappear — it turns into a pile of punchlist items, and worse, it costs you the confidence of the A-H-J. Once the authority stops trusting your work, every walk gets harder. Catching your own issues protects that trust.

---

### [SECTION 5 — Your Daily Habits]

Let me make this practical — the habits that keep you clean, day to day.

Make fire protection part of your toolbox talks, so it's on everyone's mind before they pick up a tool. Keep the clearances we covered in part one front of mind. Review your scope every single day. And walk your areas before you start work — if you spot an issue, stop the installation until it's resolved. Stopping for ten minutes now beats ripping out finished work later.

And when you find something you can't solve yourself, here's how you escalate. First, try to resolve it at the trade partner level — talk to the other trade, communicate, don't ignore it. If it needs to go up, raise it to the Hoffman construction managers, the Hoffman integrator, and the Hoffman BIM coordinators — and bring it into the work group meetings and the BIM coordination meetings. On the field side, your escalation path is the construction managers, the superintendents, and the E-H-S managers.

The pattern is always the same: try to solve it close to the work, and when you can't, push it up loudly and early. Never sit on it.

---

### [OUTRO]

And that wraps up our sprinkler head blockage training.

Let me leave you with the whole thing in three breaths. One — protect the clearances: 12 inches, the three-times rule, and the 24-by-12 zone around every head. Two — build to the I-F-F model within that one-inch tolerance, and treat every head as a live, charged device. And three — when in doubt, stop, and communicate. Every cause in this entire training traced back to somebody not speaking up. Be the person who does.

This is life-safety work, and you're the last line of defense before that A-H-J walk. Take it seriously, and you'll do great. Thanks for your attention — and stay safe out there.
