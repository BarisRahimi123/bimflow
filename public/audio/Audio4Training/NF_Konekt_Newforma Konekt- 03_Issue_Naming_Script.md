# Newforma Konekt — Part 3: Naming Your Issues
## The Issue Naming Guide — Training Script for ElevenLabs

> Bracketed headers are screen cues only — for syncing visuals. Paste ONLY the narration text underneath into ElevenLabs.

---

### [INTRO — Friendly, Welcoming Tone]

Welcome back! This is part three of our Newforma Konekt series, and it's a short one — but don't skip it, because this is the difference between issues that are a joy to manage and issues that drive everyone crazy.

In part two, you learned how to work the issues list. Today, you learn how to NAME the issues you create. A well-named issue tells the whole story at a glance, and it saves everybody hours of digging. Let's learn the formula.

---

### [SECTION 1 — Why Naming Matters]

Here's the idea. On a busy project, you might have hundreds of issues flying around. If they're named lazily — like "clash" or "problem here" — nobody knows what they mean without opening each one.

But a well-named issue communicates a lot in just a few words. Who's responsible, what the problem is, what to do about it, and where it stands. Get the name right, and people can manage it without even opening it. That's the goal.

---

### [SECTION 2 — The Format]

So here's the formula. Read it left to right:

First — the responsible subcontractors, written as SUB slash SUB, in capital letters.

Then — a short description of the issue and the suggested fix or instruction, in lowercase.

And finally — a MODIFIER in capital letters at the end, which is the status or the action flag.

The capitalization is intentional: UPPERCASE for the trades at the front, lowercase for the description in the middle, and UPPERCASE for the modifier at the end. That pattern makes each part easy to spot.

Two important rules on the trade codes. First — the very first SUB you list is the subcontractor responsible for FIXING the issue. That's a convention everyone relies on, so put the responsible party first.

Second — when an issue involves the design team, Jacobs, you use "D-M" combined with the discipline code. For example, D-M-E-zero means the design model, electrical, from Jacobs. So you'd write the responsible trade, slash, D-M-E-zero, to show it's pointing at the Jacobs electrical model.

---

### [SECTION 3 — Examples]

Let me read you a couple of real examples so the pattern clicks.

Example one: "H-M-C slash L-R-S, domestic water pipe clash through electrical tray zone, APPROVED." So — H-M-C is the responsible trade, the description is the water pipe clashing through the electrical tray zone, and the modifier APPROVED tells you it's been signed off.

Example two: "H-A-R slash D-M-E-zero, filter skids clash with electrical panel clearance, shift east 50 centimeters, R-F-I NEEDED." Here the issue points at the Jacobs electrical model, the suggested fix is to shift east 50 centimeters, and the modifier flags that an R-F-I is needed.

Hear how much information lives in one line? That's the power of good naming.

---

### [SECTION 4 — The Modifiers]

Now let's talk about those modifiers at the end — the capital-letter flags. These tell everyone the status or the next action. Let me walk you through the common ones.

For approvals, you've got APPROVED, plus more specific versions: H-C-C APPROVED and J-E-G APPROVED, depending on who signed off. And APPROVAL NEEDED when you're still waiting.

For things you're proposing, there's ACCEPTABLE, which asks "is this okay?"

For general labeling, there's T-Y-P, short for typical — meaning this condition repeats — and NOTE, for general information.

Now the R-F-I family, for requests for information: R-F-I NEEDED, R-F-I SENT, and R-F-I FOLLOWED, which walk through the lifecycle of that request.

And then the design-deviation ones, which connect to the A-9 Change Management rules: WITHIN A-9 means the deviation is inside the allowed tolerance, so no Jacobs approval is needed. J-E-G A-9 NEEDED means it's outside tolerance and you DO need Jacobs to weigh in. And UPDATE MODEL flags that the model needs to be revised.

---

### [SECTION 5 — The Question Mark Trick]

Here's one last detail that's genuinely clever. You can add a question mark to a modifier when you're ASKING something — and then remove it once it's answered.

So "APPROVED question mark" means "is this approved?" Once someone confirms it, you drop the question mark, and it simply reads "APPROVED." Same with "ACCEPTABLE question mark" and "R-F-I NEEDED question mark."

It's a tiny thing, but it lets the title itself track the back-and-forth. The question mark means "we're still waiting." No question mark means "we have our answer." Beautiful and simple.

---

### [OUTRO]

And that's issue naming. Responsible trade first, clear description in the middle, status modifier at the end — and a question mark while you're still asking.

Build this habit early and your whole team will thank you. In our final lesson, we'll cover the design issue and question workflow — how those get raised, routed, and answered. See you there.
