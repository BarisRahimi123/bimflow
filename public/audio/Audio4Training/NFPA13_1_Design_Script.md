### [INTRO — Warm, but Serious]

Hey there, and welcome. This is part one of a two-part training on sprinkler head blockage and the basics of N-F-P-A 13 — the fire sprinkler code.

I want to start by being straight with you about why this one matters more than most. Everything else we coordinate is about making the building work. This is about making the building safe. If a sprinkler can't do its job in a fire, people and equipment are at risk. So this lesson carries a little more weight — and I'd ask you to give it your full attention.

Part one, today, is about design — understanding the problem, the code behind it, and the clearances you need to protect. Part two will be about installation in the field. Let's get going.

---

### [SECTION 1 — The Problem in Plain Terms]

So what's the actual problem? It's simple to state. When utilities — pipes, conduit, ductwork, supports — get routed or installed too close to a fire sprinkler head, they block the spray.

Here's why that's serious. A sprinkler doesn't just dribble water straight down. It throws water out in a specific pattern that has to fully develop to cover the hazard. If something's sitting in the way, that pattern never forms properly, and you get gaps in coverage. And under N-F-P-A 13, that's not just sloppy — it's a code violation.

And notice — this can happen two ways. It can be designed in, when the model routes things too close. Or it can be built in, when the field install ends up too close. Either way, same result, same violation. So we have to catch it in both places.

---

### [SECTION 2 — Why It Happens]

Let me name the real causes, because if you know how blockages sneak in, you can stop them.

On the design side: sometimes it's a lack of coordination — disciplines not talking, models not checked against each other. Sometimes it's a lack of basic N-F-P-A knowledge — people just don't know the clearances. And sometimes it's attention to detail.

Now here's a line from this material I want you to really hear: "in per the design model isn't the answer." On this project, the M-E-P scope is installed from the construction model — not the design model. Hoffman and the trade partners own that construction model. So you can't point at the design and say "I followed it." The construction model is yours, and the clearances are your responsibility.

On the installation side: things aren't always fully in the 3D model — some content lives only on 2D flats. Sometimes what is modeled isn't actually buildable. And field conditions change — rebar, work installed by others. The thread running through every one of these causes is the same single word the material repeats over and over: communicate. Don't assume. Don't stay quiet. Communicate.

---

### [SECTION 3 — Who Has to Sign Off]

Let's talk about the stakes, because there's a real gate at the end of this.

Base Build is responsible for installing code-compliant fire protection in the major buildings — the fab, the P-S-S-S, the boiler-chiller plant, and the process satellite buildings. And before any of that gets turned over to Operations, the A-H-J — the Authority Having Jurisdiction, the official code authority — walks every fire protection zone for final approval and sign-off.

Think about what that means. Every blockage you leave behind is something the A-H-J can catch on that walk. All fire suppression has to comply with N-F-P-A 13 — there's no negotiating with the code. So getting it right isn't about avoiding a comment from your C-M; it's about passing the authority that lets the building open.

---

### [SECTION 4 — The Code, in Plain English]

Let me translate the key code sections into plain language. You don't need to memorize section numbers, but you should understand what they require.

The performance objective, in Section 11.3.6.1, says it simply: locate sprinklers so they minimize obstructions to the spray — or add more sprinklers to make up for it. That's the whole goal in one sentence.

Then Section 9.5.5.2 deals with obstructions to the pattern developing. The idea: an obstruction sitting within about 18 inches below the sprinkler deflector can prevent the pattern from forming, and has to be dealt with under the rules.

And Section 10.2.7.2 gives you the rule that's easiest to carry in your head — I call it the three-times rule. Unless specific exceptions apply, a sprinkler has to sit away from an obstruction by at least three times the obstruction's largest dimension. So a big obstruction needs a lot more clearance than a small one. Bigger thing in the way, farther the sprinkler has to be from it.

There's also a positioning table — the further a sprinkler is horizontally from an obstruction, the higher that obstruction's bottom can be relative to the deflector. The concept matters more than the exact rows: distance buys you forgiveness. Closer is more restrictive; farther gives you room.

---

### [SECTION 5 — What Actually Blocks Heads]

So what are we actually fighting out there? The material lists the usual suspects — the things found blocking heads in the field. M-E-P hangers, supports, and braces. M-E-P unistrut racks and vertical supports. Mechanical ductwork. Electrical conduit. L-S-S conduit. Telecom and site radio. And even fire protection piping itself.

Notice the variety — it's basically everything that runs overhead. Which means almost every trade can create a blockage. So this isn't "the other guy's problem." Whatever you route, you can be the one in the way.

---

### [SECTION 6 — The Three Clearance Rules to Remember]

Now here's the part to actually memorize. If you walk away remembering nothing else, remember these.

One — horizontal obstructions. Things running horizontally past a head are a major risk to the spray. Keep a minimum of 12 inches of clearance between the sprinkler and a horizontal obstruction.

Two — vertical obstructions. These are actually the most common type of blockage. Same rule: keep a minimum of 12 inches of clearance. And if site conditions just won't allow it, you don't fudge it — you coordinate with the fire protection trade partner.

And three — and this is the big one, the rule that ties it all together: every sprinkler gets a protected clearance zone. Picture a cylinder around each head — 24 inches in diameter and 12 inches deep — and keep it clear, no matter the orientation of the head. Nothing routes into that zone. If you protect that 24-by-12 cylinder around every head, you've solved most of this problem before it starts.

One more to watch: diagonal seismic bracing. It can block a head too — and it's sneaky, because it's sometimes missing from the model and shows up as a field addition. The rule there is to be diligent and get that bracing into the construction model so it actually gets coordinated.

---

### [SECTION 7 — The Tight Spots]

Two areas deserve extra care because space is brutally tight.

At the utility level, there are defined zones — a vertical clearance zone and a horizontal routing zone — laid out so the sprinkler "halo" stays protected while everything else routes around it. The total fire protection zone runs roughly 13 to 15 inches depending on the main size. The point: that space is planned, not random. Respect the zones.

And at the SUBFAB level, under the waffle slab, you've got L-S-S, telecom, site radio, and fire protection all fighting for the same cramped space. Locating and spacing the heads there, and coordinating with everyone else, is absolutely critical — because there's almost no room for error.
