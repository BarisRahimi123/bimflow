### [INTRO — Friendly, Welcoming Tone]

Welcome back! This is part two of our BIM closeout lesson.

In part one, you got your files truly ready — clean, named, reconciled, and purged. Today we actually submit them and follow them through the review. I'll show you how to package a submittal, how to name it so it tracks properly, and what happens at each gate — including what to do when something gets sent back. Let's go.

---

### [SECTION 1 — A Couple of Ground Rules First]

Before the steps, two limits that keep the review process from jamming up.

First — don't dump huge batches into one review. As a general rule, keep a single review to around 20 files or fewer unless a coordinator has approved more. Second — there's usually a cap on how many reviews you fire off at once — commonly no more than two reviews per day, per trade partner, per building. These limits exist so reviewers can actually keep up. Blow past them and you risk getting your whole review voided.

And one more thing to carry through this entire lesson: if any required item fails, the review can be voided — by your internal BIM team or by the owner, depending on how far it got. So precision matters at every step.

---

### [SECTION 2 — Packaging the Submittal]

Now let's submit. The core action is selecting your files and choosing "Submit for Review." But here's the part to get exactly right — remember from part one, source and coordination files travel together.

For AutoCAD, that means selecting the D-W-G and its N-W-C at the same time, before you submit. Post both, select both.

For Revit, it means the R-V-T plus all of the N-W-Cs associated with that model, all in one review. Often your model file and your coordination files live in different folders, so after you start the review, you use the "Add documents" link to go grab the associated N-W-Cs and pull them in. Don't submit the Revit file naked — it needs its N-W-Cs alongside it.

And here is a rule I want to underline hard: you cannot close out a portion of a Revit file. That means every level and all the scope inside that Revit model has to be complete before you submit it. Revit closeout is all-or-nothing. If half the model is still in progress, the whole file waits.

---

### [SECTION 3 — Naming the Review]

Every submittal gets a structured review name, and consistency here is what lets everyone track your closeout through the system.

The exact format is set by your project, but it's built from predictable pieces — things like your company code, the building, the level, and a running submittal number. The important concept is that running number — often called the ticker. It counts up as you submit, and typically each trade starts its ticker at the beginning for each building.

Now here's the rule about that ticker that genuinely matters, so listen closely. If your submittal gets voided internally — by your own BIM team, before it ever reaches the owner — you can reuse the same ticker number on your corrected resubmittal. But if it makes it all the way to the owner's audit and gets rejected there, you must use a brand-new ticker number on the resubmittal. Internal bounce-back, reuse the number. Owner rejection, fresh number. That distinction keeps the record clean about how far each attempt got.

---

### [SECTION 4 — The Review Workflow]

Okay — you've submitted. What happens now? Let me walk you through the journey, because closeout is really a series of gates.

It starts with you: you initiate the closeout review.

Gate one is the internal BIM review. Your BIM team reviews the submittal against the checklist and either approves or rejects it. If they reject it, it comes back to you — you fix the issues and resubmit, reusing that same ticker number we just talked about. This is the internal bounce-back loop.

If it passes the internal review, it moves to gate two — the audit, often performed on your source files by the owner's side, with the results posted back into the system. The auditor asks the same basic question: does it pass?

If the audit rejects it, it comes back to you again — and this time, remember, you resubmit under a new ticker number, because it reached the owner.

If the audit approves it — you're done. Your files get locked in the source folder, the tracker gets updated, and the approved files are transferred to the owner's document management system as the official record.

So the path is: you initiate, internal BIM reviews, owner audits, and at the end your files are locked and archived. Two gates, two possible bounce-backs, and a clear finish line.

---

### [SECTION 5 — How to Read a Rejection]

Let me say a quick word about rejections, because they feel bad and they shouldn't.

A rejection isn't a failure of you — it's the system doing exactly its job, catching something before it becomes a permanent, archived mistake. The whole point of closeout is that these are the final files of record. They might be referenced for years. So a careful reviewer who sends something back is protecting that record — and protecting you.

When you get one, the move is simple: read the results they linked, fix precisely what they flagged, mind the ticker rule for which number to use, and resubmit. Calm, specific, done. No drama.

---

### [OUTRO]

And that completes BIM closeout, start to finish.

Let's recap the whole thing in a breath. In part one you prepared clean, complete, reconciled files. Today you packaged them correctly — source and coordination files together, full Revit files only — named them with a trackable ticker, and walked them through the internal review and the owner audit until they're locked and archived. And you learned the ticker rule: reuse it on an internal bounce, new number after an owner rejection.

Get your files clean, submit them as complete sets, and respond to feedback calmly — and closeout becomes the satisfying final step it's meant to be: your work, finished, accepted, and on the record. Nice work getting through both parts — go close it out.
